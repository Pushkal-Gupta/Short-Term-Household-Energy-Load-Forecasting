\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{What heterogeneity costs, and what it does not}
The usual explanation for FedAvg's shortfall in household forecasting is that homes are too different. Our
controlled experiment does not support that explanation for final accuracy. Holding everything else fixed, doubling
the load-shape divergence between clients changed FedAvg's test error by at most 0.0012\,kWh, comparable to the
seed-to-seed variation.
The fragmentation sweep shows where the gap does come from. The federated protocol with one client matches
centralised training, and the error then grows with the logarithm of the number of clients the same data are spread
over, whether or not those clients differ.
Heterogeneity did matter for the speed of convergence: the traffic needed to approach centralised accuracy grew by
about 85\,\% from random to clustered grouping. In practice this means that heterogeneity should be budgeted as a
communication and latency cost. It is not a reason to expect a worse final model, provided that the round budget is
large enough and the best round is chosen on validation data.

This also explains why remedies aimed at client drift showed little benefit here. FedProx helped only when its
coefficient was close to zero, and the adaptive variant performed worse. When fragmentation, rather than drift between
heterogeneous clients, causes most of the gap, a drift penalty has little to correct and mainly slows local progress.

\subsection{Implications for deployment}
The most consequential design choice in our experiments was the number of clients the data were spread over.
Aggregating homes behind a building gateway, a community energy hub or a supplier-side aggregator, and federating
those ($K=5$ here), cost \EonefourfedavgKfiveGap{}\,\% in MAE relative to centralised training and needed a quarter of
the traffic of 20 clients (\EonefourfedavgKfiveMB{}\,MB). Meter-level federation ($K=100$) lost
\EonethreefedavghouseholdGap{}\,\% and moved about 30 times the raw data. Where governance allows an intermediate
tier, it is the cheapest accuracy lever available.

At this scale, federation is a governance choice rather than a bandwidth saving. Eight months of half-hourly training
readings from one household occupy about 140\,kB, roughly twice a single message of our small LSTM. Thirty rounds of
federated training therefore move several times more data than uploading the readings would, and even the traffic
needed to reach near-centralised accuracy is of the same order as the data. The case for FL in residential
forecasting rests on data minimisation, consent and regulation, not on network load. This agrees with the
qualitative argument of Briggs et al.~\cite{briggs2020ccai} and qualifies the analytic estimates of large
savings~\cite{taik2020,savi2021}, which depend on model size, sampling rate and the number of rounds.

Two further steps are cheap and worth taking. One epoch of local fine-tuning costs no communication, closed much of
the per-household gap between FedAvg and local training, and cut the share of homes harmed by federation from about
two thirds to under one half; we would not deploy a federated household forecaster without it. The round should also
be chosen on validation error. Validation error rose for a while in the early rounds of every federated
configuration, so a short fixed budget can deliver a worse model than a longer one with validation-based selection.

Finally, operators should look at the high-consumption tail. The homes that gain most from pooled data are the
largest consumers, and they are also the ones FedAvg serves worst. A distribution system operator using federated
forecasts for congestion management should therefore evaluate errors per consumption tier and not only on average.

\subsection{On adaptive proximal coefficients}
HA-FedProx was designed on the premise that the dispersion of client updates measures heterogeneity. The premise is
half right. The dispersion ratio does rank partitions by heterogeneity, but within a run it is dominated by the
convergence of the global model: as the shared descent direction shrinks, any residual disagreement, including pure
stochastic-gradient noise, makes the ratio approach one. A schedule proportional to $\rho_t$ therefore increases the
proximal pull late in training regardless of the data. Two corrections suggest themselves: normalising $\rho_t$ by
its value under a random partition, which requires a reference the server does not have, or using only early-round
dispersion, which our data show is the most heterogeneity-sensitive. We leave both to future work. Our main
recommendation is a caution: dissimilarity estimates computed from client updates (here, an update-space version of
FedProx's $B$-dissimilarity) confound heterogeneity with the stage of optimisation, and any rule that adapts
regularisation to them should be checked against this confound.

\subsection{Limitations}
The study covers one dataset, one city, one year and one horizon: a winter test period and 30-minute-ahead forecasts
for 100 standard-tariff London homes. Its conclusions concern a small LSTM. Larger models raise per-message traffic in
proportion, which would strengthen the communication finding but might change the accuracy results.

The network and the stragglers are simulated, and traffic counts payload bytes only (protocol overhead was measured
at about 1\,\%). Keeping data local is not a formal privacy guarantee: updates can leak
information~\cite{zhu2019leakage,geiping2020inverting}, and we applied neither differential privacy nor secure
aggregation. Both would add accuracy or communication costs on top of those reported here.

The learning rate, model size and input window were fixed in advance and shared by all arms; only the proximal
coefficients were tuned. A per-arm search could move individual numbers, although the controlled design should make
such shifts affect all arms alike. Lastly, for efficiency the simulator scores the global model on every household's
validation and test windows centrally. In a deployment the clients would compute these scores and report only the
metrics.
