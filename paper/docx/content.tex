\title{Fragmentation outweighs heterogeneity in the accuracy cost of federated household load forecasting}

\begin{abstract}
Federated learning (FL) is proposed for household load forecasting because meter readings stay at home. Its accuracy loss is usually blamed on differences between households, and its bandwidth advantage is
assumed. This study separated heterogeneity, data fragmentation and training budget on 100 London households from the
Low Carbon London trial, with fixed homes, test period and long short-term memory forecaster. The homes were split over
1 to 100 clients in several ways, including a control that dealt training windows, not homes, to clients so that
clients did not differ; heterogeneity was measured and every byte counted. With 30 rounds, the gap to centralised
training grew from \RvHomeKfiveGap{}\,\% at five clients to \RvHomeKhundredGap{}\,\% with one home per client, and the
control kept \RvWinKhundredShare{}\,\% of that gap. Measured heterogeneity added a smaller, significant cost; over the
tested ranges, fragmentation accounted for about four times as much error. Training for up to 100 rounds narrowed the
gap at 100 clients to \RvLongKhundredGap{}\,\% without closing it, and the pattern held in two further cohorts.
Federated training moved more bytes than the raw readings in any encoding. With 20 clients of Dirichlet-mixed homes
($\alpha=0.3$), federated averaging left \RvHarmFedModerateMargin{}\,\% of households more than 1\,\% worse off than
local models, and local fine-tuning reduced this to \RvHarmFedModerateFTMargin{}\,\%. Without fine-tuning, federation
was not significantly better than letting each client train alone on its pooled homes. Calendar inputs and proximal
regularisation did not help. Federation here is a governance choice, not a bandwidth saving.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Short-term forecasts of single-household load feed demand response, home energy management, local flexibility
markets and low-voltage network operation. Long short-term memory (LSTM) networks~\cite{hochreiter1997lstm} forecast individual homes better than
per-home statistical models when they are trained on many households at once~\cite{kong2019stlf,shi2018pdrnn}, but
pooling the readings is exactly what smart-meter regulation and customer consent discourage, because half-hourly
consumption reveals occupancy and daily routines.

Federated learning (FL)~\cite{mcmahan2017fedavg,kairouz2021advances} lets each meter, gateway or community hub train
locally and send only model weights to an aggregator. Its use for residential load forecasting is
growing~\cite{fernndez2022,fekri2022,gholizadeh2022,widmer2023,qu2023,liu2023,das2026}, built on two premises: that
household data are so heterogeneous (non-IID) that plain federated averaging (FedAvg) needs remedies such as
FedProx~\cite{li2020fedprox}, clustering~\cite{gholizadeh2022,dogra2023} or personalisation~\cite{qu2023,widmer2023},
and that FL is the communication-light option~\cite{taik2020,savi2021}.

Neither premise has been tested under controlled conditions: studies rarely hold the households and test set fixed while
varying only how the data are split. Three causes of the accuracy lost by FL are therefore entangled: heterogeneity between clients, the
fragmentation of the data into many small clients, and the optimisation budget, since smaller clients take fewer local
steps per round. Bandwidth claims rest on analytic estimates~\cite{taik2020,briggs2020ccai}. This paper separates these factors on \NumHouseholds{} homes of the Low Carbon
London (LCL) trial~\cite{ukpn2015lcl}, with an independent and identically distributed (IID) control that fragments the
data without making clients different, an extended training budget, and two further cohorts.

The principal contributions of this work are as follows:
\begin{itemize}
\item A controlled decomposition of the federated--centralised gap with an IID control and an extended budget, showing
that most of the gap is a cost of fragmenting the data: clients that did not differ kept \RvWinShareLo{}--\RvWinShareHi{}\,\%
of it, while measured heterogeneity added a smaller, significant share and longer training narrowed but did not close it.
\item A byte ledger of federated training against four encodings of the raw readings, showing that federation of a
small LSTM moves more data than it keeps local, and that the traffic effect of heterogeneity is small and depends on the
threshold used to measure it.
\item A household-level analysis of who loses from federation, with a 1\,\% margin and a client-only baseline,
showing that federation between community hubs pays off only with local fine-tuning, which also removes most of the
harm to individual homes.
\item Negative results for calendar inputs and for proximal regularisation, whose validation-tuned coefficient was zero.
\end{itemize}

\section{Literature Review}

\subsection{Federated household load forecasting}
Pooling neighbouring households reduces over-fitting in deep household forecasters~\cite{shi2018pdrnn}; whether
federation preserves that benefit is disputed. On 100 LCL
households, Briggs et al.~\cite{briggs2022} found plain FedAvg 7.6\,\% worse in RMSE than localised learning, with
clustering reducing the shortfall to 4.7\,\% and local fine-tuning reversing it; in the same study their centralised
model was 4.8\,\% worse than localised learning. Gholizadeh and Musilek~\cite{gholizadeh2022} also found local
learning best on average, whereas Fekri et al.~\cite{fekri2022} found FedAvg comparable to, or better than, per-meter
models on 19 homes. Personalised and clustered variants~\cite{qu2023,widmer2023,dogra2023,das2026,lu2024fds} are almost
always motivated by household heterogeneity.

\subsection{Communication cost of federation}
Communication efficiency was a founding motivation of FL~\cite{mcmahan2017fedavg,konecny2016comm}, but for load
forecasting the evidence points both ways. Taïk and Cherkaoui~\cite{taik2020} estimated analytically that FL saves
90--97\,\% of the network load of uploading data, and Savi and Olivadese~\cite{savi2021} reported up to 50 times less
exchanged data, whereas Briggs et al.~\cite{briggs2020ccai} argued without measurement that weight vectors cost more
than half-hourly readings on smart-meter links.

\subsection{Non-IID data, local steps and proximal regularisation}
Statistical heterogeneity slows and biases FedAvg~\cite{zhao2018noniid,kairouz2021advances}. Remedies include proximal
regularisation (FedProx~\cite{li2020fedprox}), control variates (SCAFFOLD~\cite{karimireddy2020scaffold}), adaptive
server optimisers~\cite{reddi2021fedopt} and personalisation, and heterogeneity is usually induced with a Dirichlet
split over class labels~\cite{hsu2019dirichlet}, which has no direct analogue for regression. A separate line of work
on local stochastic gradient descent shows that, even with IID data, the error of averaged local updates depends on the
number of local steps between averaging and on how many workers share the
data~\cite{stich2019localsgd,woodworth2020localsgd,woodworth2020minibatch}. Splitting a fixed data set over more
clients changes exactly these quantities, so a growing federated gap need not indicate heterogeneity at all. The
FedProx coefficient is usually tuned by hand, with adaptive variants based on the training loss~\cite{li2020fedprox} or a
performance index~\cite{mhou2025hapi}.

\subsection{Research gap}
The closest studies used LCL~\cite{briggs2022}, London Hydro~\cite{fekri2022}, EPCOR~\cite{gholizadeh2022} and Pecan
Street~\cite{taik2020} homes. None separates heterogeneity from fragmentation and budget while holding households and
test set fixed, and none counts bytes during training against the raw-data volume.


\section{Methodology}

\subsection{Problem formulation}
Let $x_{i,t}$ be the energy (kWh) used by household $i$ in half-hour $t$, standardised with statistics from that
household's training period. From the last $L=48$ readings the model predicts the next half-hour,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:task}
\hat{x}_{i,t+1}=f_w\left(x_{i,t-L+1},\ldots,x_{i,t}\right),
\end{equation}
where $f_w$ is a single-layer LSTM with 64 hidden units and a linear output ($P=\NumParams{}$ parameters,
\PayloadKB{}\,kB in 32-bit floats), trained with mean squared error and Adam (learning rate $10^{-3}$, batch 256). In
the federated arms, $K$ clients hold disjoint subsets of the training windows; in round $r$ the server sends the global
weights $w_r$ to a set of clients, each trains for $E$ local epochs, and the returning set $S_r'$ is averaged:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fedavg}
w_{r+1}=\sum_{k\in S_r'}p_k w_k,\qquad p_k=\frac{n_k}{\sum_{j\in S_r'}n_j},
\end{equation}
where $n_k$ is the number of training windows of client $k$~\cite{mcmahan2017fedavg}. Clients are stateless: each local
update starts a fresh Adam optimiser from $w_r$, so no optimiser moments are carried between rounds.

\subsection{Data}
The public LCL release~\cite{ukpn2015lcl} contains half-hourly consumption of 5{,}567 London households. For calendar
year 2013, standard-tariff households with at least 95\,\% coverage were eligible (\HouseholdsEligible{} homes); the
time-of-use group was excluded because it received dynamic prices that year. The main cohort of \NumHouseholds{} homes
was drawn with a fixed seed, stratified by quintile of mean load; two further cohorts were drawn the same way with other
seeds and share only three or four homes with it. Invalid or implausible readings (above 10\,kWh per half-hour) were
set to missing, gaps of up to 2\,h were interpolated, and windows touching longer gaps were dropped. The split was
chronological for every home: training January--August, validation September--October and test November--December
2013 (1{,}160{,}952 training and 292{,}052 test windows in the main cohort). Scalers, profile clusters and the
heterogeneity index used training data only, and every model-selection decision used validation data only. The data
are public and anonymised.

\subsection{Arms and partitions}
Fig.~\ref{fig:workflow} summarises the study. Persistence ($\hat{x}_{t+1}=x_t$) and seasonal naive
($\hat{x}_{t+1}=x_{t-47}$) forecasts served as references. Local-only training fitted one model per household and
centralised training one model to the pooled windows of all households; both ran for at most 30 epochs with early
stopping on validation MAE (patience 4). A client-only arm let each federated client train alone on the homes it
holds, with the same early stopping. FedAvg followed
Eq.~\eqref{eq:fedavg}; FedProx added $\frac{\mu}{2}\lVert w-w_r\rVert^2$ to each local loss~\cite{li2020fedprox};
and FT fine-tuned the final global model for one epoch on each household's own data, at no communication cost.

With one home per client, $K=100$. Otherwise the same homes were grouped into $K$ equal-sized clients of $100/K$
homes, as behind a building gateway or community energy hub. Each household was described by its normalised daily
load shape and log mean load and clustered with $k$-means into $G=5$ profile clusters. Five groupings of homes were
used. \emph{Random} grouping permuted the homes and cut the permutation into $K$ blocks. \emph{Dirichlet} grouping
drew for each client a mixture $\pi\sim\mathrm{Dir}(\alpha\mathbf{1}_G)$ and filled each of its slots with a random home
from a cluster drawn from $\pi$, renormalised over clusters with homes left~\cite{hsu2019dirichlet}. \emph{Cluster}
grouping sorted homes by cluster and mean load and cut the list into blocks. \emph{Shape} grouping sorted homes by the
first principal component of their hour-of-week profile and cut the list into blocks, the largest difference in load
shape that equal-sized clients of these homes allow. Finally, the \emph{window} control was not a grouping of homes:
the pooled training windows of all homes were dealt at random into $K$ equal shards, so that every client saw the same
mixture of homes. It fragments the data exactly as household grouping does while keeping clients statistically
identical, so any gap it shows is due to fragmentation and budget, not heterogeneity. Validation and test errors were
always computed per household on the same windows.

\textbf{Measured heterogeneity.} With $q_k$ the distribution of client $k$'s training consumption over the 168 hours of
the week and JS the Jensen--Shannon divergence with base-2 logarithms~\cite{lin1991divergence}, bounded in $[0,1]$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:h}
H_{\mathrm{JS}}={\binom{K}{2}}^{-1}\sum_{k<l}\mathrm{JS}\left(q_k\Vert q_l\right).
\end{equation}
$H_{\mathrm{JS}}$ measures differences in when clients consume. Differences in how much they consume were measured
separately by the coefficient of variation of client mean load, $H_{\mathrm{CV}}$; the two were not combined, because a
composite would be dominated by the level term (it made up \RvHcvShare{}\,\% of the earlier composite index).

\textbf{Communication.} A message carries $P\cdot b$ bytes ($b=4$ for 32-bit, 2 for 16-bit updates), with one
download per selected client and one upload per returning client; framing headers are excluded. With $v_r$ the
validation MAE after round $r$ and $v^\star$ that of centralised training, R@$\varepsilon$ is the first round with
$v_r\le(1+\varepsilon)v^\star$ and B@$\varepsilon$ the bytes exchanged up to it, for
$\varepsilon\in\{5,10,15,20\}\,\%$. Because any single threshold is arbitrary, the mean excess
$\frac{1}{30}\sum_r(v_r/v^\star-1)$ over the first 30 rounds was also reported. The raw-data volume was counted for
four encodings: 2- and 4-byte readings, a 12-byte reading with a 4-byte timestamp offset and a 4-byte household index
(the figure used for centralised traffic), and the public comma-separated file. Stragglers (probability 0.2) completed
a random fraction of their local work; FedAvg dropped them, FedProx kept their partial updates~\cite{li2020fedprox}.

\subsection{Experimental setup}
The default federated setting used $K=20$, full participation and $R=30$ rounds of $E=1$ epoch, the same number of
passes over the data as the centralised budget. The experiments varied $K$ (1, 5, 20, 50, 100), the grouping (window,
random, $\alpha\in\{1,0.3,0.1\}$, cluster and shape), participation ($C=0.5$ with stragglers), the aggregation rule and
16-bit updates. The local budget per round shrinks with $K$: a client took \RvStepsKone{} Adam steps per round at $K=1$,
\RvStepsKtwenty{} at $K=20$ and \RvStepsKhundred{} at $K=100$. FedAvg at $K=20$, 50 and 100 was therefore rerun for up
to 100 rounds, stopped after 25 rounds without a new best validation MAE. Two further checks addressed the forecaster:
sine and cosine encodings of time of day and day of week as extra inputs, and the mean absolute scaled error (MASE),
scaled per household by the in-sample one-step naive MAE on its training data. Each configuration ran with three seeds;
the heterogeneity, participation and precision configurations ran with five. A seed fixed the model initialisation, the
random grouping or Dirichlet draw, client sampling and stragglers, and the order of mini-batches; the cohort was fixed.
The proximal coefficients were tuned on validation data over $\mu\in\{0,10^{-4},10^{-3},10^{-2},10^{-1}\}$.
The pipeline used Python 3.14.7, PyTorch 2.14.1 and an in-process simulator whose clients follow the Flower interface;
Flower 1.39.0~\cite{beutel2020flower} reproduced its results exactly.

Accuracy was measured by MAE, RMSE and weighted absolute percentage error (WAPE), micro-averaged over all test
half-hours, and by MASE. For paired tests, each household's MAE was first averaged over seeds; arms were then compared
with Wilcoxon signed-rank tests over the 100 households~\cite{wilcoxon1945}, with Holm correction~\cite{holm1979}
applied over the whole family of comparisons in Supplementary Table S1. A household was counted as harmed by an arm if
its MAE was more than 1\,\% above its local-only MAE. Results with $p_{\mathrm{Holm}}\ge0.05$ are described as not
significant, without implying equivalence.

\subsection{Main arms}
Table~\ref{tab:main} gives the test error of the main arms. Centralised training was the most accurate arm, better than
persistence by \RvCentralVsPersist{}\,\% in MAE, and better than local-only training for
\StEtwocentralizedvsEonelocalBetter{}\,\% of households ($p_{\mathrm{Holm}}$ \pv{\StEtwocentralizedvsEonelocalP}).
Not every federated arm beat the naive references: with one home per client, FedAvg was slightly worse than persistence

\subsection{Fragmentation and training budget}
The gap to centralised training appeared as soon as the homes were split and grew with the number of clients
(Fig.~\ref{fig:frag} and Table~\ref{tab:frag}), from \RvHomeKfiveGap{}\,\% at $K=5$ to \RvHomeKtwentyGap{}\,\% at $K=20$,

splitting also makes clients more different: $H_{\mathrm{JS}}$ rose from \RvHJSHomeKfive{} at $K=5$ to
\RvHJSHomeKhundred{} at $K=100$, because a smaller client averages over fewer homes. The window control removed this
confound. With windows dealt at random, $H_{\mathrm{JS}}$ stayed at or below \RvWinKhundredHJS{} (the residual is the
sampling noise of finite shards) and client mean loads were equal, yet the gap remained \RvWinKfiveGap{}, \RvWinKtwentyGap{},
\RvWinKfiftyGap{} and \RvWinKhundredGap{}\,\% at $K=5$, 20, 50 and 100, that is \RvWinShareLo{}--\RvWinShareHi{}\,\%

\subsection{Heterogeneity at a fixed number of clients}
At $K=20$, grouping the homes from random to cluster almost doubled $H_{\mathrm{JS}}$ (Table~\ref{tab:het}), and
cluster grouping also raised the spread of client mean load. Final test error hardly moved: across the groupings of
homes it stayed within \RvHetMaxDiff{}\,kWh, the 95\,\% intervals overlapped, and the rank correlation between
$H_{\mathrm{JS}}$ and test MAE over runs was \RvHetSpearman{} ($p$ \RvHetSpearmanP{}, $n=\RvHetSpearmanN{}$). Per
household, cluster grouping was not significantly different from random grouping ($p_{\mathrm{Holm}}$
\pv{\StEsixfedavgstrongvsEthreefedavgiidP}). The window control was the most accurate grouping
(\RvHetwindowMAE{}\,kWh), consistent with the small heterogeneity term of the regression. Sorting homes by load shape gave the largest divergence
($H_{\mathrm{JS}}=\RvHetshapesortedHJS{}$) and the same error (\RvHetshapesortedMAE{}\,kWh), so the absence of an effect
is not an artefact of the cluster definition; it does, however, hold only over the narrow range of heterogeneity
that equal-sized clients of these homes allow.

The traffic needed to approach centralised accuracy depended more on the threshold and the seed than on the grouping
(Fig.~\ref{fig:het}). Within 15 or 20\,\% of centralised validation error, every run of every grouping qualified after the
first round (\RvHetrandomBonefive{}\,MB). Within 10\,\%, cluster grouping needed \RvHetBepsIncrease{}\,\% more traffic than random grouping
on average over five seeds, but the IID control needed more than random grouping too (\RvHetwindowBonezero{} against
\RvHetrandomBonezero{}\,MB), so this difference is within seed variation. Within 5\,\%, many runs never qualified in 30
rounds. The threshold-free excess error over the first 30 rounds rose steadily but modestly, from \RvHetwindowExcess{}\,\%
for the window control and \RvHetrandomExcess{}\,\% for random grouping to \RvHetclusterExcess{}\,\%, so heterogeneity
slowed FedAvg slightly. These traffic results concern FedAvg; the proximal variants converged differently
(Supplementary Section S3).

\subsection{Forecaster strength, client-only training and other cohorts}
Calendar inputs did not strengthen the forecaster (Supplementary Table S8): centralised MAE went from
\EtwocentralizedMAE{} to \RvCalCentralisedmaecal{}\,kWh, and with one home per client FedAvg became worse

\subsection{Communication}
One message was \PayloadKB{}\,kB, so 30 rounds with $K=20$ moved \EthreefedavgiidMB{}\,MB and household clients moved

\section{Discussion}

Most of the accuracy cost of federation in this setting came from fragmentation. Splitting the same data over more
clients cost accuracy even when the clients were statistically identical, as described for local stochastic gradient descent:
smaller shards give each client fewer steps between averages, and the average of many short local trajectories
progresses less per round than one pass over the pooled data~\cite{stich2019localsgd,woodworth2020localsgd,woodworth2020minibatch}. Part of this cost is recoverable with more rounds, at
proportionally more traffic. Heterogeneity was a real but smaller cost, and
it mainly slowed convergence. This is why drift remedies helped little here. The heterogeneity reachable with these homes was limited ($H_{\mathrm{JS}}$ at most \RvHJSHomeKhundred{}),
and populations with photovoltaics, electric vehicles or heat pumps may differ much more, so the relative weight of the
two costs should not be generalised beyond London households of this kind.

In practice, the number of clients was the most consequential choice. Federating five aggregated clients cost
\RvHomeKfiveGap{}\,\% in MAE, meter-level federation \RvHomeKhundredGap{}\,\% with 30 rounds and \RvLongKhundredGap{}\,\%
with up to 100. Since one household's eight months of training readings occupy about 47\,kB even at 4 bytes per value,
less than one 68.9\,kB message, federation at this scale is a governance choice based on data minimisation and consent,
not a bandwidth saving. Operators should federate few, aggregated clients, budget rounds by local steps rather than epochs,
fine-tune locally and check errors per consumption tier.

The results agree with Briggs et al.~\cite{briggs2022} that plain FedAvg can lose to local models on LCL, and that
local fine-tuning recovers most of the loss. They differ on centralised training, which was the best arm here but
4.8\,\% worse than localised learning in their Table~II. Their models were smaller (two layers of 20 units), used hourly
sequences of 6 to 24 steps and trained for up to 500 epochs, and their centralised error was averaged over sequences
rather than per client. The measured traffic supports the argument of Briggs et al.~\cite{briggs2020ccai} rather than the analytic
savings of Taïk and Cherkaoui~\cite{taik2020}, who compared a model of ``1.9\,kb'' with 16\,Mb of data. Their two LSTM
layers of 200 units imply roughly $4.8\times10^5$ weights, about 1.9\,MB in 32-bit floats, so the printed unit is
probably a slip; with that correction their model would be of the same order as their data, as here.

Pooling homes into community clients requires a trusted holder, such as a building gateway, an energy community
or a supplier that already reads the meters; an outside aggregator is not one. Where such a holder exists, the client-only results show that it gains little from
federating with other hubs unless the shared model is fine-tuned locally. Where no intermediary is trusted, only the
one-home-per-client setting applies, and there the fragmentation cost is largest.

The study covers one dataset and year, one horizon and a small LSTM. The forecaster is close to
persistence (MASE near one), as is typical for single homes at this resolution, so absolute gains are small. The
network was simulated, traffic counts payload only, and data locality is not a formal privacy
guarantee~\cite{zhu2019leakage,geiping2020inverting}: neither differential privacy nor secure aggregation was applied.
Hyper-parameters other than the proximal coefficients and the number of rounds were fixed for all arms, and server-side
adaptive optimisers~\cite{reddi2021fedopt} were not tested.

\section{Conclusion}

Holding households, test set and model fixed on 100 Low Carbon London homes and two further cohorts, this study found
that the error of federated household load forecasting grew with the number of clients the same homes were spread over, to \RvHomeKhundredGap{}\,\% when every home was its own
client, and most of that growth remained when clients received random shares of the data and did not differ at all.
Measured heterogeneity added a smaller cost and slightly slowed convergence; training for longer narrowed but did not
close the gap. Federated training moved more bytes than the readings it kept local, and one epoch of local fine-tuning
removed most of the harm to individual households at community scale, while calendar inputs and proximal
regularisation brought no benefit. The federated accuracy gap in this setting is mainly a fragmentation and budget cost,
and the case for federation in residential forecasting rests on governance rather than bandwidth. Future work will test
more heterogeneous populations, other horizons and larger models, and add differential privacy and secure aggregation.
