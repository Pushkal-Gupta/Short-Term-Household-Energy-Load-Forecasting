\title{Beyond Data Heterogeneity: Training Budget and Fragmentation in Federated Household Load Forecasting}

\begin{abstract}
Federated learning (FL) is proposed for household load forecasting because meter readings stay at home. Its accuracy loss
is usually blamed on differences between households, and its bandwidth advantage is assumed. This study separated
heterogeneity, data fragmentation and training budget on 100 London households from the Low Carbon London trial with a
fixed one-step long short-term memory forecaster. The homes were split over 1 to 100 clients, including a control that
dealt training windows, not homes, to clients so that clients did not differ. After 30 rounds, the gap to centralised
training grew from \RvHomeKfiveGap{}\,\% at five clients to \RvHomeKhundredGap{}\,\% with one home per client, and with 20
or more clients the control kept \RvCoShareBigLo{}--\RvCoShareBigHi{}\,\% of the gap in three cohorts. Training to a
plateau, at about \RvPlhomesKhundredMBtotal{}\,MB per run on average (\RvPlhomesKhundredCapMB{}\,MB for the run stopped at the round cap), shrank the gap to \RvPlHomeGapLo{}--\RvPlHomeGapHi{}\,\% for
homes and \RvPlWinGapLo{}--\RvPlWinGapHi{}\,\% for the control: much of the cost was training budget, and part remained
without heterogeneity. Over 100 rounds, server-side Adam narrowed the one-home-per-client gap from
\RvOptAdamKonezerozeroGap{} to \RvOptFedAdamKonezerozeroGap{}\,\%; kept client optimiser state and client SGD did not
help. With 20 or more clients, federated training moved more bytes than the raw readings in every encoding tested.
With one home per client, federated averaging with local fine-tuning (\EonethreefedavghouseholdFT{}\,kWh mean absolute
error) still lost to local models (\EonelocalMAE{}\,kWh). For one-step forecasts, federation here is a governance
choice, not a bandwidth saving.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Short-term forecasts of single-household load feed demand response, home energy management, local flexibility
markets and low-voltage network operation. Long short-term memory (LSTM) networks~\cite{hochreiter1997lstm} forecast individual homes better than
per-home statistical models when they are trained on many households at once~\cite{kong2019stlf,shi2018pdrnn}, but
pooling the readings is exactly what smart-meter regulation and customer consent discourage, because half-hourly
consumption reveals occupancy and daily routines.

Federated learning (FL)~\cite{mcmahan2017fedavg,kairouz2021advances} lets each meter, gateway or community hub train
locally and send only model weights to an aggregator. Its use for residential load forecasting is
growing~\cite{fernndez2022,fekri2022,gholizadeh2022,widmer2023,qu2023,liu2023,das2026}, built on two premises: that
household data are so heterogeneous (non-IID) that plain federated averaging (FedAvg) needs remedies such as
FedProx~\cite{li2020fedprox}, clustering~\cite{gholizadeh2022,dogra2023} or personalisation~\cite{qu2023,widmer2023},
and that FL is the communication-light option~\cite{taik2020,savi2021}.

Neither premise has been tested under controlled conditions: studies rarely hold the households and test set fixed while
varying only how the data are split. Three causes of the accuracy lost by FL are therefore entangled: heterogeneity between clients, the
fragmentation of the data into many small clients, and the optimisation budget, since smaller clients take fewer local
steps per round. Bandwidth claims rest on analytic estimates~\cite{taik2020,briggs2020ccai}. This paper separates these factors on \NumHouseholds{} homes of the Low Carbon
London (LCL) trial~\cite{ukpn2015lcl}, with an independent and identically distributed (IID) control that fragments the
data without making clients different, training to a plateau, three client and server optimisers, and two further
cohorts.

The principal contributions of this work are as follows:
\begin{itemize}
\item A controlled decomposition of the federated--centralised gap with an IID control trained to a plateau, showing
that most of the gap after a fixed budget is a cost of fragmenting the data and of the budget itself: with 20 or more
clients, clients that did not differ kept \RvWinShareLo{}--\RvWinShareHi{}\,\% of the 30-round gap
(\RvWinKfiveShare{}\,\% at $K=5$), training to a plateau removed much but not all of it, and measured heterogeneity
added a smaller share.
\item A byte ledger of federated training against four encodings of the raw readings, showing that federation of a
small LSTM with 20 or more clients moves more data than it keeps local, and that the traffic effect of heterogeneity is small and depends on the
threshold used to measure it.
\item A household-level analysis of who loses from federation, with a 1\,\% margin and a client-only baseline,
showing that federation between community hubs improves on client-only training, if at all, only with local fine-tuning
(consistently across seeds but not significantly after correction), which lowers the share of harmed
homes from \RvHarmFedModerateMargin{} to \RvHarmFedModerateFTMargin{}\,\% at $K=20$ and from \RvHarmFedKonezerozeroMargin{}
to \RvHarmFedKonezerozeroFTMargin{}\,\% at $K=100$ without removing it.
\item Negative results for calendar inputs, for proximal regularisation, whose validation-tuned coefficient was zero, and
for client-side optimiser changes, against a partial gain from a server-side adaptive optimiser.
\end{itemize}

\section{Literature Review}

\subsection{Federated household load forecasting}
Pooling neighbouring households reduces over-fitting in deep household forecasters~\cite{shi2018pdrnn}; whether
federation preserves that benefit is disputed. On 100 LCL
households, Briggs et al.~\cite{briggs2022} found plain FedAvg 7.6\,\% worse in RMSE than localised learning, with
clustering reducing the shortfall to 4.7\,\% and local fine-tuning reversing it; in the same study their centralised
model was 4.8\,\% worse than localised learning. Gholizadeh and Musilek~\cite{gholizadeh2022} also found local
learning best on average, whereas Fekri et al.~\cite{fekri2022} found FedAvg comparable to, or better than, per-meter
models on 19 homes. Personalised and clustered variants~\cite{qu2023,widmer2023,dogra2023,das2026,lu2024fds} are almost
always motivated by household heterogeneity, and recent work continues in that direction with shared encoders~\cite{zhu2025pfholo},
meta-learning with control variates~\cite{feng2025fmaml}, adaptive or metaheuristic
aggregation~\cite{abdulla2024,sarantinopoulos2025}, fairness between participants~\cite{wang2025mgdflp}, personalised
building models~\cite{wang2024pflbuildings,dissem2025} and secure or attack-tolerant training~\cite{husnoo2024,mostafanezhad2025}.
A recent benchmark found that once every household fine-tunes the shared model, clustering and personalisation end within
2\,\% of each other, with clustering needing about a third of the rounds~\cite{zheng2026benchmark}.

\subsection{Communication cost of federation}
Communication efficiency was a founding motivation of FL~\cite{mcmahan2017fedavg,konecny2016comm}, but for load
forecasting the evidence points both ways. Taïk and Cherkaoui~\cite{taik2020} estimated analytically that FL saves
90--97\,\% of the network load of uploading data, and Savi and Olivadese~\cite{savi2021} reported up to 50 times less
exchanged data, whereas Briggs et al.~\cite{briggs2020ccai} argued without measurement that weight vectors cost more
than half-hourly readings on smart-meter links. The advantage reported by Savi and Olivadese was tied to sampling finer
than one hour, and both savings figures compare a model with the data volume rather than counting the bytes of a
training run.

\subsection{Non-IID data, local steps and proximal regularisation}
Statistical heterogeneity slows and biases FedAvg~\cite{zhao2018noniid,kairouz2021advances}. Remedies include proximal
regularisation (FedProx~\cite{li2020fedprox}), control variates (SCAFFOLD~\cite{karimireddy2020scaffold}), adaptive
server optimisers~\cite{reddi2021fedopt} and personalisation, and heterogeneity is usually induced with a Dirichlet
split over class labels~\cite{hsu2019dirichlet}, which has no direct analogue for regression. A separate line of work
on local stochastic gradient descent shows that, even with IID data, the error of averaged local updates depends on the
number of local steps between averaging and on how many workers share the
data~\cite{stich2019localsgd,woodworth2020localsgd,woodworth2020minibatch}. Splitting a fixed data set over more
clients changes exactly these quantities, so a growing federated gap need not indicate heterogeneity at all. The
FedProx coefficient is usually tuned by hand, with adaptive variants based on the training loss~\cite{li2020fedprox} or a
performance index~\cite{mhou2025hapi}.

\subsection{Research gap}
The closest studies used LCL~\cite{briggs2022}, London Hydro~\cite{fekri2022}, EPCOR~\cite{gholizadeh2022} and Pecan
Street~\cite{taik2020} homes. None separates heterogeneity from fragmentation and budget while holding households and
test set fixed, and none counts bytes during training against the raw-data volume.


\section{Methodology}

\subsection{Problem formulation}
Let $x_{i,t}$ be the energy (kWh) used by household $i$ in half-hour $t$, standardised with statistics from that
household's training period. From the last $L=48$ readings the model predicts the next half-hour,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:task}
\hat{x}_{i,t+1}=f_w\left(x_{i,t-L+1},\ldots,x_{i,t}\right),
\end{equation}
where $f_w$ is a single-layer LSTM with 64 hidden units and a linear output ($P=\NumParams{}$ parameters,
\PayloadKB{}\,kB in 32-bit floats), trained with mean squared error and Adam (learning rate $10^{-3}$, batch 256). In
the federated arms, $K$ clients hold disjoint subsets of the training windows; in round $r$ the server sends the global
weights $w_r$ to a set of clients, each trains for $E$ local epochs, and the returning set $S_r'$ is averaged:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fedavg}
w_{r+1}=\sum_{k\in S_r'}p_k w_k,\qquad p_k=\frac{n_k}{\sum_{j\in S_r'}n_j},
\end{equation}
where $n_k$ is the number of training windows of client $k$~\cite{mcmahan2017fedavg}. By default clients are
stateless: each local update starts a fresh Adam optimiser from $w_r$. Three alternatives were tested: client Adam
state kept across rounds, plain client stochastic gradient descent (SGD), and FedAdam~\cite{reddi2021fedopt}, which
applies Adam on the server to the averaged update $\sum_k p_k w_k-w_r$ ($\beta_1=0.9$, $\beta_2=0.99$,
$\tau=10^{-3}$). The SGD and server step sizes were chosen on validation MAE (Supplementary Table S7).

\subsection{Data}
The public LCL release~\cite{ukpn2015lcl} contains half-hourly consumption of 5{,}567 London households. For calendar
year 2013, standard-tariff households with at least 95\,\% coverage were eligible (\HouseholdsEligible{} homes); the
time-of-use group was excluded because it received dynamic prices that year. The main cohort of \NumHouseholds{} homes
was drawn with a fixed seed, stratified by quintile of mean load; two further cohorts were drawn the same way with other
seeds and share only three or four homes with it. Invalid or implausible readings (above 10\,kWh per half-hour) were
set to missing, gaps of up to 2\,h were interpolated, and windows touching longer gaps were dropped. The split was
chronological for every home: training January--August, validation September--October and test November--December
2013 (1{,}160{,}952 training and 292{,}052 test windows in the main cohort). Scalers, profile clusters and the
heterogeneity index used training data only, and every model-selection decision used validation data only. The data
are public and anonymised.

\subsection{Arms and partitions}
Fig.~\ref{fig:workflow} summarises the study. Persistence ($\hat{x}_{t+1}=x_t$) and seasonal naive
($\hat{x}_{t+1}=x_{t-47}$) forecasts served as references. Local-only training fitted one model per household and
centralised training one model to the pooled windows of all households; both ran for at most 30 epochs with early
stopping on validation MAE (patience 4). A client-only arm let each federated client train alone on the homes it
holds, with the same early stopping. FedAvg followed
Eq.~\eqref{eq:fedavg}; FedProx added $\frac{\mu}{2}\lVert w-w_r\rVert^2$ to each local loss~\cite{li2020fedprox};
and FT fine-tuned the final global model for one epoch on each household's own data, at no communication cost.

With one home per client, $K=100$. Otherwise the same homes were grouped into $K$ equal-sized clients of $100/K$
homes, as behind a building gateway or community energy hub. Each household was described by its normalised daily
load shape and log mean load and clustered with $k$-means into $G=5$ profile clusters. Four groupings of homes were
used, together with one control. \emph{Random} grouping permuted the homes and cut the permutation into $K$ blocks. \emph{Dirichlet} grouping
drew for each client a mixture $\pi\sim\mathrm{Dir}(\alpha\mathbf{1}_G)$ and filled each of its slots with a random home
from a cluster drawn from $\pi$, renormalised over clusters with homes left~\cite{hsu2019dirichlet}. \emph{Cluster}
grouping sorted homes by cluster and mean load and cut the list into blocks. \emph{Shape} grouping sorted homes by the
first principal component of their hour-of-week profile and cut the list into blocks, the largest difference in load
shape that equal-sized clients of these homes allow. Finally, the \emph{window} control was not a grouping of homes:
the pooled training windows of all homes were dealt at random into $K$ equal shards, so that every client saw the same
mixture of homes. It fragments the data exactly as household grouping does while keeping clients statistically
identical, so any gap it shows is due to fragmentation and budget, not heterogeneity. Validation and test errors were
always computed per household on the same windows.

\textbf{Measured heterogeneity.} With $q_k$ the distribution of client $k$'s training consumption over the 168 hours of
the week and JS the Jensen--Shannon divergence with base-2 logarithms~\cite{lin1991divergence}, bounded in $[0,1]$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:h}
H_{\mathrm{JS}}={\binom{K}{2}}^{-1}\sum_{k<l}\mathrm{JS}\left(q_k\Vert q_l\right).
\end{equation}
$H_{\mathrm{JS}}$ measures differences in when clients consume. Differences in how much they consume were measured
separately by the coefficient of variation of client mean load, $H_{\mathrm{CV}}$; the two were not combined, because a
composite would be dominated by the level term (it made up \RvHcvShare{}\,\% of $H_{\mathrm{JS}}+H_{\mathrm{CV}}/(1+H_{\mathrm{CV}})$; Supplementary Table S3).

\textbf{Communication.} A message carries $P\cdot b$ bytes ($b=4$ for 32-bit, 2 for 16-bit updates), with one
download per selected client and one upload per returning client; framing headers are excluded. With $v_r$ the
validation MAE after round $r$ and $v^\star$ that of centralised training, R@$\varepsilon$ is the first round after
which $v_r\le(1+\varepsilon)v^\star$ held for every remaining round of the run, so that a transient dip does not
count, and B@$\varepsilon$ the bytes exchanged up to it, for
$\varepsilon\in\{5,10,15,20\}\,\%$. Because any single threshold is arbitrary, the mean excess
$\frac{1}{30}\sum_r(v_r/v^\star-1)$ over the first 30 rounds was also reported. The raw-data volume was counted for
four encodings: 2- and 4-byte readings, a 12-byte reading with a 4-byte timestamp offset and a 4-byte household index
(the figure used for centralised traffic), and the public comma-separated file. Stragglers (probability 0.2) completed
a random fraction of their local work; FedAvg dropped them, FedProx kept their partial updates~\cite{li2020fedprox}.

\subsection{Experimental setup}
The default federated setting used $K=20$, full participation and $R=30$ rounds of $E=1$ epoch, the same number of
passes over the data as the centralised budget. The experiments varied $K$ (1, 5, 20, 50, 100), the grouping (window,
random, $\alpha\in\{1,0.3,0.1\}$, cluster and shape), participation ($C=0.5$ with stragglers), the aggregation rule and
16-bit updates. The local budget per round shrinks with $K$: a client took \RvStepsKone{} Adam steps per round at $K=1$,
\RvStepsKtwenty{} at $K=20$ and \RvStepsKhundred{} at $K=100$. FedAvg with homes and with the window control was
therefore also trained to a plateau at $K=20$, 50 and 100: up to 500 rounds, stopped after 50 rounds without a new best
validation MAE. The optimisers were compared at $K=20$ and 100 over 100 rounds, stopped after 25 rounds without
improvement. Two further checks addressed the forecaster:
sine and cosine encodings of time of day and day of week as extra inputs, and the mean absolute scaled error (MASE),
scaled per household by the in-sample one-step naive MAE on its training data. Each configuration ran with three seeds;
the arms in the main comparisons and the heterogeneity, participation and precision configurations ran with five. A seed fixed the model initialisation, the
random grouping or Dirichlet draw, client sampling and stragglers, and the order of mini-batches; the cohort was fixed.
The proximal coefficients were tuned on validation data over $\mu\in\{0,10^{-4},10^{-3},10^{-2},10^{-1}\}$.
The pipeline used Python 3.14.7, PyTorch 2.14.1 and an in-process simulator whose clients follow the Flower interface;
Flower 1.39.0~\cite{beutel2020flower} reproduced its results exactly.

Accuracy was measured by MAE, RMSE and weighted absolute percentage error (WAPE), micro-averaged over all test
half-hours, and by MASE. Households scored by one trained model are not independent, so the training run was the unit
of replication: arms were compared on the pooled test MAE of each run, paired by seed where both had the same seeds
(paired $t$-test) and with Welch's test otherwise. With every household in every run this is equivalent to testing the
arm in a linear mixed model with crossed random effects for household and run. One family of \RvTsFamily{} comparisons
(Supplementary Table S1) was corrected with Holm's method~\cite{holm1979}, and every $p$-value in the text is this
corrected value unless marked otherwise; differences $\Delta$ are given in $10^{-4}$\,kWh with 95\,\% confidence
intervals. The share of households that did better under an arm is descriptive only. A household was counted as harmed
by an arm if its MAE was more than 1\,\% above its local-only MAE. Results with $p_{\mathrm{Holm}}\ge0.05$ are described
as not significant, without implying equivalence.

\subsection{Fragmentation and training budget}
The gap to centralised training appeared as soon as the homes were split and grew with the number of clients
(Table~\ref{tab:frag}), from \RvHomeKfiveGap{}\,\% at $K=5$ to \RvHomeKtwentyGap{}\,\% at $K=20$,
\RvHomeKfiftyGap{}\,\% at $K=50$ and \RvHomeKhundredGap{}\,\% with one home per client after 30 rounds; at run level the
gaps at $K=20$ ($\Delta=\RvTsKtwentyCenDiff{}$, $p_{\mathrm{Holm}}$\,\RvTsKtwentyCenP{}) and $K=100$
($\Delta=\RvTsKhundredCenDiff{}$, $p_{\mathrm{Holm}}$\,\RvTsKhundredCenP{}) were significant. Under random
grouping, however, splitting also makes clients more different: $H_{\mathrm{JS}}$ rose from \RvHJSHomeKfive{} at $K=5$ to
\RvHJSHomeKhundred{} at $K=100$, because a smaller client averages over fewer homes. The window control removed this
confound. With windows dealt at random, $H_{\mathrm{JS}}$ stayed at or below \RvWinKhundredHJS{} (the sampling noise of
finite shards) and client mean loads were equal, yet the gap remained \RvWinKfiveGap{}, \RvWinKtwentyGap{},
\RvWinKfiftyGap{} and \RvWinKhundredGap{}\,\% at $K=5$, 20, 50 and 100, that is \RvWinShareLo{}--\RvWinShareHi{}\,\% of
the gap with homes at $K\ge20$ and \RvWinKfiveShare{}\,\% at $K=5$ (shares from unrounded gaps). At run level the
windows were better than homes by $\Delta=\RvTsWinHomeKtwentyDiff{}$ at $K=20$ and \RvTsWinHomeKhundredDiff{} at
$K=100$, neither significant ($p_{\mathrm{Holm}}$\,\RvTsWinHomeKtwentyP{} at $K=20$ and $p_{\mathrm{Holm}}$\,\RvTsWinHomeKhundredP{} at $K=100$).

A regression of run-level test MAE on $\ln K$, $H_{\mathrm{JS}}$ and an indicator for groupings of homes, over all FedAvg
runs with random, Dirichlet, cluster, shape and window partitions ($n=\RvRegTwoN{}$), gave $\RvRegTwolnK{}\times10^{-3}$\,kWh
per unit of $\ln K$ (95\,\% CI \RvRegTwolnKlo{} to \RvRegTwolnKhi{}, $p$\,\RvRegTwolnKP{}),
$\RvRegTwoHJS{}\times10^{-3}$\,kWh per unit of $H_{\mathrm{JS}}$ (CI \RvRegTwoHJSlo{} to \RvRegTwoHJShi{}, $p$\,\RvRegTwoHJSP{})
and $\RvRegTwohomes{}\times10^{-3}$\,kWh for homes rather than windows (CI \RvRegTwohomeslo{} to \RvRegTwohomeshi{},
$p$\,\RvRegTwohomesP{}; uncorrected). Among groupings of homes $\ln K$ and $H_{\mathrm{JS}}$ were correlated
($r=\RvRegCorrKH{}$), so the pooled fit cannot fully separate them; within $K=20$ alone, the $H_{\mathrm{JS}}$ term was

\subsection{Optimiser set-up}
Restarting Adam in every round is a design choice, so the 100-round runs at $K=20$ and $K=100$ were repeated with
three alternatives (Supplementary Table S6). Keeping the client Adam state gave a test MAE of
\RvOptAdamKeepKtwozeroMAE{} against \RvOptAdamKtwozeroMAE{}\,kWh at $K=20$ ($\Delta=\RvTsKeepKtwentyDiff{}$,
$p_{\mathrm{Holm}}$\,\RvTsKeepKtwentyP{}) and \RvOptAdamKeepKonezerozeroMAE{} against \RvOptAdamKonezerozeroMAE{}\,kWh at
$K=100$ ($\Delta=\RvTsKeepKhundredDiff{}$, $p_{\mathrm{Holm}}$\,\RvTsKeepKhundredP{}). Client SGD with the step size
chosen on validation (\RvGridsgdBest{}) reached \RvOptSGDKtwozeroMAE{} and \RvOptSGDKonezerozeroMAE{}\,kWh, and FedAdam
with server step size \RvGridfedadamBest{} reached \RvOptFedAdamKtwozeroMAE{} and \RvOptFedAdamKonezerozeroMAE{}\,kWh
($\Delta=\RvTsFedAdamKtwentyDiff{}$ and \RvTsFedAdamKhundredDiff{} against Adam with restarts). Keeping the
optimiser state therefore made no difference, and client SGD was worse at both sizes, significantly so at $K=20$
($p_{\mathrm{Holm}}$\,\RvTsSgdKtwentyP{}). FedAdam did not help at $K=20$, but with one home per client it narrowed the
gap from \RvOptAdamKonezerozeroGap{} to \RvOptFedAdamKonezerozeroGap{}\,\% in \RvTsFedAdamKhundredSeeds{} paired seeds,
selecting its model by round \RvOptFedAdamKonezerozeroBest{} on average, about as close as plain FedAvg came after
hundreds of rounds (\RvPlhomesKhundredGap{}\,\% at the plateau); the difference was not significant with three seeds ($p_{\mathrm{Holm}}$\,\RvTsFedAdamKhundredP{}).
The step size 0.03, run because 0.01 lay at the edge of the grid and almost tied with it on validation, gave
\RvOptFedAdamOtherKonezerozeroGap{}\,\% at $K=100$ and \RvOptFedAdamOtherKtwozeroGap{}\,\% at $K=20$; it is reported but
was not selected. A server-side adaptive optimiser thus recovers part of the budget cost of meter-level federation, but
no optimiser closed the gap.

\subsection{Heterogeneity at a fixed number of clients}
At $K=20$, grouping the homes from random to cluster almost doubled $H_{\mathrm{JS}}$ (Table~\ref{tab:het}), and
cluster grouping also raised the spread of client mean load. Final test error hardly moved: across the groupings of
homes it ranged from \RvHetMaeLo{} to \RvHetMaeHi{}\,kWh, the 95\,\% intervals overlapped, and the rank correlation between
$H_{\mathrm{JS}}$ and test MAE over runs was \RvHetSpearman{} ($p$\,\RvHetSpearmanP{}, uncorrected, $n=\RvHetSpearmanN{}$).
Cluster grouping did not differ significantly from random grouping ($\Delta=\RvTsCluRndDiff{}$, CI \RvTsCluRndLo{} to
\RvTsCluRndHi{}, $p_{\mathrm{Holm}}$\,\RvTsCluRndP{}). The window control was the most accurate grouping
(\RvHetwindowMAE{}\,kWh). Sorting homes by load shape gave the largest divergence ($H_{\mathrm{JS}}=\RvHetshapesortedHJS{}$)
and the same error (\RvHetshapesortedMAE{}\,kWh), so the absence of an effect is not an artefact of the cluster
definition; it holds, however, only over the narrow range of heterogeneity that equal-sized clients of these homes allow.

Heterogeneity showed up early in training rather than at the end. After the first round, validation error rose for a
few rounds before falling (Fig.~\ref{fig:het}a): the largest rise above its running minimum in the first ten rounds was
\RvRisewindow{}\,\% for the window control and \RvRiseHomeLo{}--\RvRiseHomeHi{}\,\% for groupings of homes. In the same
rounds the client updates disagreed far more when clients held different homes (mean normalised disagreement over
rounds 2--5 of \RvRhoEarlyHomeLo{}--\RvRhoEarlyHomeHi{}, against \RvRhoEarlywindow{} for the control; Supplementary
Eq.~S1): each client's first epochs pull the shared model towards its own profiles, and the average of these divergent
models is temporarily worse. Restarting Adam each round did not cause the rise: with the optimiser state kept, it was
\RvOptAdamKeepKtwozeroRise{}\,\% at $K=20$, against \RvOptAdamKtwozeroRise{}\,\% with restarts over the same seeds.

The traffic needed to settle near centralised accuracy depended more on the threshold and the seed than on the grouping
(Fig.~\ref{fig:het}b). Within 20\,\%, the control settled after the first round in every seed
(\RvHetwindowBtwozero{}\,MB), whereas seeds of the groupings of homes needed between \RvHetrandomBtwozeroLo{} and
\RvHetclusterBtwozeroHi{}\,MB. Within 10\,\%, the control needed the least traffic on average
(\RvHetwindowBonezero{}\,MB; \RvHetwindowBonezeroLo{}--\RvHetwindowBonezeroHi{}\,MB over seeds). Among groupings of homes the means ranged from
\RvHetshapesortedBonezero{}\,MB for shape-sorted grouping, the most divergent, to \RvHetclusterBonezero{}\,MB for cluster
grouping, with random grouping at \RvHetrandomBonezero{}\,MB, and their seed ranges overlapped widely (random
\RvHetrandomBonezeroLo{}--\RvHetrandomBonezeroHi{}, cluster \RvHetclusterBonezeroLo{}--\RvHetclusterBonezeroHi{}\,MB), so
traffic did not follow $H_{\mathrm{JS}}$. Within 5\,\%, \RvHetwindowBfiveN{} of \RvHetwindowN{} control runs settled within 30 rounds, against
\RvHetazerothreeBfiveN{} run at $\alpha=0.3$, \RvHetazerooneBfiveN{} at $\alpha=0.1$ and none with the other groupings of
homes, all of them near the end of the 30 rounds. The threshold-free excess error over the
first 30 rounds was \RvHetwindowExcess{}\,\% for the control and \RvHetExcessHomeLo{}--\RvHetExcessHomeHi{}\,\% for the
groupings of homes, not ordered by $H_{\mathrm{JS}}$: heterogeneity slowed FedAvg slightly, without a dose--response
over this range. These results concern FedAvg; the proximal variants converged differently (Supplementary Section S3).

\subsection{Communication}
One message was \PayloadKB{}\,kB, so 30 rounds with $K=20$ moved \EthreefedavgiidMB{}\,MB and household clients moved
\EonethreefedavghouseholdMB{}\,MB (Table~\ref{tab:main}). The eight months of training readings of the cohort
(\RvRawReadings{} million values) occupy \RvRawTwo{}\,MB at 2 bytes per reading, \RvRawFour{}\,MB at 4 bytes,
\RvRawTwelve{}\,MB at 12 bytes and \RvRawCsv{}\,MB in the public comma-separated format (about \RvCsvBytesPerRow{}
bytes per row). Thirty rounds at $K\ge20$ with 32-bit updates and full participation thus moved more bytes than the
readings in any of these encodings. Settings with less traffic, $K=5$ (\EonefourfedavgKfiveMB{}\,MB), 16-bit updates
(\EonetwofedavgfponesixmoderateMB{}\,MB) and half participation with stragglers (\EeightfedavgpartialMB{}\,MB), moved
more than the binary encodings but less than the comma-separated file: 16-bit updates halved the traffic and half participation with stragglers
lowered it further. Neither lost accuracy: against full participation with 32-bit updates, the pooled MAE changed by
\RvPrecfponesixRel{}\,\% with 16-bit updates ($\Delta=\RvTsFponesixDiff{}$, $p_{\mathrm{Holm}}$\,\RvTsFponesixP{}; \RvTsFponesixHH{}\,\% of households
did better) and by \RvPrecpartialRel{}\,\% with half participation ($\Delta=\RvTsPartialDiff{}$, $p_{\mathrm{Holm}}$\,\RvTsPartialP{};
\RvTsPartialHH{}\,\% of households did better). Both plausibly act as mild regularisers. Training to a plateau multiplies traffic by the number of rounds
(see the plateau runs above), so at these settings the bandwidth comparison with the raw readings only becomes less favourable to
federation with longer training.

\section{Discussion}

After a fixed budget, most of the accuracy cost of federation in this setting came from fragmentation and from the
budget itself. Splitting the same data over more clients cost accuracy even when the clients were statistically
identical, as described for local stochastic gradient descent: smaller shards give each client fewer steps between
averages, and the average of many short local trajectories progresses less per round than one pass over the pooled
data~\cite{stich2019localsgd,woodworth2020localsgd,woodworth2020minibatch}. Training to a plateau recovered a large part
of this cost, at a traffic of gigabytes per run, but not all of it, and the part that remained with identical clients
was of the same order as the part attributable to heterogeneity. Heterogeneity mainly slowed early convergence, through
disagreeing client updates, which may explain why drift remedies helped little here, whereas a server-side adaptive optimiser,
which acts on the budget, narrowed the meter-level gap. The heterogeneity
reachable with these homes was limited ($H_{\mathrm{JS}}$ at most \RvHJSHomeKhundred{}), and populations with
photovoltaics, electric vehicles, heat pumps or time-of-use tariffs, or clients of unequal size, may differ much more,
so the relative weight of the costs should not be generalised beyond London households of this kind.

In practice, the number of clients and the budget were the most consequential choices. Federating five aggregated
clients cost \RvHomeKfiveGap{}\,\% in MAE after 30 rounds; meter-level federation cost \RvHomeKhundredGap{}\,\% after 30
rounds and \RvPlhomesKhundredGap{}\,\% at the plateau, after about \RvPlhomesKhundredMBtotal{}\,MB of traffic per run on average (\RvPlhomesKhundredCapMB{}\,MB for the capped run). Since one
household's eight months of training readings occupy about 47\,kB even at 4 bytes per value, less than one 68.9\,kB
message, federation at this scale is a governance choice based on data minimisation and consent, not a bandwidth
saving. Operators should federate few, aggregated clients held by a trusted hub such as a building gateway or energy
community, budget rounds by local steps rather than epochs, fine-tune locally and check errors per consumption tier;
a hub gains little from federating with other hubs unless the shared model is fine-tuned on its homes.

The results agree with Briggs et al.~\cite{briggs2022} that plain FedAvg can lose to local models on LCL, and that
local fine-tuning recovers most of the loss. They differ on centralised training, which was the best arm here but
4.8\,\% worse than localised learning in their Table~II. Their models were smaller (two layers of 20 units), used hourly
sequences of 6 to 24 steps and were trained for up to 500 epochs, and their centralised error was averaged over
sequences rather than per client. A budget that large is consistent with the present finding that much of the federated
gap is a budget cost. The measured traffic supports the argument of Briggs et al.~\cite{briggs2020ccai} rather than the
analytic savings of Taïk and Cherkaoui~\cite{taik2020}, whose ``1.9\,kb'' model (two LSTM layers of 200 units, about
1.9\,MB in 32-bit floats) was probably a unit slip and would be of the same order as their 16\,Mb of data.

The study covers one dataset and year, standard-tariff homes in equal-sized clients, one-step forecasts and a small LSTM,
and its claims are limited to that horizon; day-ahead forecasting with rolling evaluation was not tested. The forecaster
is close to persistence (MASE near one), as is typical for single homes at this resolution, so absolute gains are small.
Three to five seeds per arm gave little power at run level, so several differences that were consistent in direction were not
significant after correction. The network was simulated, traffic counts payload only, and data locality is not a formal
privacy guarantee~\cite{zhu2019leakage,geiping2020inverting}: neither differential privacy nor secure aggregation was
applied.

\section{Conclusion}

Holding households, test set and model fixed on 100 Low Carbon London homes and two further cohorts, this study found
that the error of federated household load forecasting grew with the number of clients the same homes were spread
over, to \RvHomeKhundredGap{}\,\% after 30 rounds when every home was its own client, and that most of that growth
remained when clients received random shares of the data and did not differ at all. Training to a plateau removed much
of the gap at a large traffic cost, but not all of it, even for identical clients; heterogeneity added a smaller cost
and slowed early convergence. A server-side adaptive optimiser recovered part of the budget cost with one home per
client; keeping client optimiser state or using client SGD did not. With 20 or more clients, training moved more bytes than the readings
it kept local, and local fine-tuning reduced, but did not remove, the share of harmed households. Calendar inputs and
proximal regularisation brought no benefit. For one-step forecasts, the federated gap here is mainly a cost of budget
and fragmentation, and the case for federation in residential forecasting rests on governance rather than bandwidth.
Future work will test more heterogeneous populations and unequal clients, day-ahead horizons with rolling evaluation,
larger models, and differential privacy and secure aggregation.
